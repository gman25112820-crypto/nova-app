# What belongs in Fabulously Me — a research brief

**For:** Helen & Daisy (clinical review)
**Prepared:** July 2026
**Purpose:** Ground the next phase of activity/content design in the evidence base, so the rooms hold genuinely useful, distinct activities rather than repeated versions of the same handful. This is a starting point for a design conversation, not a finished spec.

## The one-line summary
The app already has the right instincts — low-demand, no streaks, no guilt, warmth over correction. Those instincts line up closely with what the evidence says works for anxious autistic, ADHD and PDA children. What is missing is a distinct, evidence-anchored set of activities distributed so each room does a different job. Four established frameworks map onto the rooms that already exist. The main risk to design around is the thing the closest comparable app got criticised for: activities that quietly become demands, or framing that invalidates a childs feelings.

## Part 1 — Frameworks worth building on (ordered by fit)

### 1. The Interoception Curriculum (Kelly Mahler) — strongest fit for body/feelings rooms
Interoception is the sense of the bodys internal state — a racing heart, tense shoulders, tiredness, the flutter before something goes wrong. For many autistic and ADHD children these signals are missed, misread, or overwhelming, and that difficulty is directly linked to trouble recognising and managing emotions (related to alexithymia). Evidence is unusually good: a 2022 study of a 25-week programme found significant improvements in BOTH interoceptive awareness AND emotion regulation in autistic children, and the two were related. Fits the app because it is curiosity-based, not demand-based — "notice what your body feels like right now", no correct answer. Maps to My Body and How I Feel. Activity: body-signal check (tap where you notice something, optionally what it means for YOU — not a fixed dictionary); "body detective" experiments (do a small thing, notice what changed).

### 2. Adapted CBT for autistic children — strong evidence, use the tools not the pressure
Best-established evidence base for reducing anxiety in this population; what Molehill Mountain is built on. Useful low-demand pieces: worry externalising (get a worry OUT of the head into a container the child controls — Molehill Mountains best-liked tool lets children "lock away" a worry and choose when to take it out); naming the worrys type (sensory / social / specific / general); light optional thought-and-feeling logging over time. Avoid full "challenge your unhelpful thought" exercises — they can feel like being told your feelings are wrong. Keep the tools, drop the correction.

### 3. Zones of Regulation — useful vocabulary, handle the known critique
Most widely used emotional-regulation framework in schools: four colour-coded zones. 2024 edition adopted neurodiversity-affirming language. THE CRITIQUE MATTERS HERE: in practice Zones often gets used to push children OUT of red/yellow and BACK to green ("ready to learn"), sending the message that some feelings are bad and calm-looking is the goal — the opposite of the apps PDA-safe rule that all feelings are allowed and the child never performs disappointment. Borrow the idea that emotions come with energy levels (underpins the parked "energy as second axis" idea). Do NOT build anything that nudges a child from one state to another or treats calm as the win. Evidence note: broad SEL evidence is stronger than evidence for Zones as a specific named programme.

### 4. Low-demand / PDA-affirming approach — the operating system, not a feature
The frame everything sits inside, and the apps real differentiator. Demand avoidance is anxiety-driven, a need for autonomy — not defiance. Standard autism strategies (incl. ABA-style demands-and-consequences) can make PDA worse. What helps: reducing/rephrasing demands, genuine choice (2-3 options child picks), declarative not imperative language ("I wonder if..." not "Now do..."), processing time, playfulness, collaboration over compliance. Concretely: every activity is an invitation never a task; offer a small choice at entry; no completion pressure; declarative labels.

## Part 2 — Comparable apps

### Molehill Mountain (Autistica / Kings College London) — closest evidence-led comparator
Built on adapted CBT. Loop: daily check-in, log worries (with type), "worry mountain" lock-away tool, three end-of-day questions, optional diary, unlock a tip, optional share with a trusted supporter, dashboard of change over time. Borrow: the worry-externalising lock-away tool (most praised), worry-typing, optional sharing, slow unlocking. Design AROUND its criticised failures: the name/framing invalidated real fears ("molehill" felt dismissive); tiny input fields and typos broke trust (clarity is clinical, not cosmetic for autistic users); it is aimed at 16+/adults — leaving a real gap for a warm child-facing version, which is where Fabulously Me sits.

### Broader landscape
Most autism apps cluster into: AAC, visual schedules, emotion-recognition games, behaviour trackers, calm/sensory tools. Recurring findings: many apps are NOT actually built on evidence-based practice; clinical mental-health apps frequently fail on engagement (users find them irrelevant/untrustworthy/a chore). Implications: being genuinely evidence-anchored is a real differentiator; engagement through warmth and autonomy (not streaks/pressure) is the right bet and is forced by the PDA-safe rules anyway.

## Part 3 — Mapping onto existing rooms
The house has the right rooms; give each a distinct evidence-anchored purpose so no two feel the same (fixes the duplication problem).
- My Body -> Interoception (body-signal check, body detective)
- How I Feel -> Interoception + emotion-naming (feeling check with energy level)
- My Battery -> energy/arousal awareness (whats your energy, what tops it up/drains it)
- Feeling Better -> adapted-CBT tools + calm (worry externalising, breathing offered not required)
- Good Things -> positive affect / savouring (notice one good thing, no obligation)
- Games Room / Living Room -> play, co-regulation, connection (genuinely DIFFERENT games per room)
- Calm Pond / Rest Nest -> sensory down-regulation (no goal)
Distribution principle: each activity lives in ONE natural home. Variety comes from spreading existing activities correctly before building new ones.

## Part 4 — Design guard rails (non-negotiable, evidence-backed)
1. All feelings are allowed. Nothing ranks, corrects, or nudges toward a "better" state.
2. Never minimise a worry. No "its only small" framing.
3. Everything is an invitation. No tasks, no "next", no due, no completion pressure.
4. Offer choice at entry. 2-3 options the child picks between.
5. Clarity is clinical. Clear, polished, unambiguous UI.
6. The personal map beats the universal rule. Let children build their own meanings.
7. A trusted adult is a feature not surveillance. Optional child-controlled sharing, never automatic reporting.

## Part 5 — Open questions for Helen & Daisy
1. How much structure vs pure free-play? (Interoception evidence comes from a structured 25-week curriculum.)
2. Energy as a second axis — worth building into How I Feel / My Battery, or over-complicates?
3. Worry externalising for children — safe and useful here, and how to frame so it never feels dismissive?
4. Age range — interoception evidence skews 9-19; much PDA guidance is younger. What band are we designing for?
5. What counts as "enough" activities before more becomes pressure/clutter?
6. Which frameworks do you two want to anchor to?
